Message from the Founder

I still remember the first time I interpreted a meeting with overseas investors. I was nervous, trying to catch every word and get the numbers right. Even converting Japanese financial figures into billions of yen took concentration.

As I interpreted more meetings, I began to understand what investors were trying to work out. They asked about segment margins, growth in product sales, prices and volumes because they were building their own forecasts. They wanted to know how the business might perform over the next year or two, and how their expectations compared with management's plans.

Translating analyst reports and earnings calls gave me a better understanding of how investors think. Before translating a call, I would listen to the whole recording, often while walking around my neighborhood. I paid attention to how management explained the business, what analysts asked, and where each answer led the conversation.

Over time, I grew used to the way analysts and management spoke to each other. That helped me understand what investors were getting at in meetings, and why they followed up on particular points.

I have now interpreted more than 300 meetings with overseas investors. The discussion often goes beyond what the company has disclosed. If sales are expected to grow, investors ask whether that means higher volumes, price increases or new products. If margins are improving, they ask about product mix, production costs and fixed costs. Those answers feed into their earnings forecasts.

Understanding the business and the investor's questions helps me keep up with the discussion. My job is to make sure each side understands what the other is saying. I keep my own views out of it and focus on conveying each person's meaning accurately. Both sides need to trust that I am representing their views fairly, especially when they disagree.

I learned the importance of tone through a mistake. In one meeting, I let an investor's frustration with the company's answers come through in my own voice. That upset the management team. Since then, I have made a point of keeping my tone calm while conveying the question in full. If a discussion gets heated, I sometimes slow down.

JII grew out of my writing about Japanese small-cap stocks on social media. Overseas investors who read my posts began asking for help speaking with companies. Arranging a meeting could be difficult, especially when a company had no analyst coverage. So I began contacting companies, setting up meetings and interpreting the conversations.

As more investors approached me, I founded Japan Investor Interface in 2023. Today, I arrange and interpret investor meetings and translate IR materials and corporate disclosures into English.

My aim is to help investors ask the questions they need answered and give companies the chance to explain their businesses clearly. A good meeting should leave both sides with a clearer understanding of each other.

Teddy Okuyama
Representative Director
Japan Investor Interface Co., Ltd.
