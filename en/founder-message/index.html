Message from the Founder

I still remember my first meeting as an interpreter for overseas investors. I was nervous, concentrating on every word and checking the zeros as I converted Japanese financial figures into billions. Keeping up with the conversation took all my attention.

With experience, I began to understand what investors were trying to work out. They asked about segment margins, product growth, prices and volumes because they were building their own forecasts. They wanted to understand how the business might perform over the next year or two, and how their expectations compared with management's plans.

Translating analyst reports and earnings calls helped me learn to follow that thinking. Before working on a transcript, I would listen to the entire recording, often while walking around my neighborhood. I paid attention to how management explained the business, what analysts asked, and where each answer led the conversation.

Over time, those exchanges became familiar. In investor meetings, I could follow the purpose of a question and understand why the next one arose.

I have now interpreted more than 300 meetings with overseas investors. Often, the discussion gets into details beyond the published materials. If sales are expected to grow, investors want to know how much comes from volume, pricing or new products. If margins are improving, they ask about product mix, costs and the burden of fixed expenses. Those answers help them form their own view of the company's earnings.

Understanding the business and the investor's questions helps me follow these conversations accurately. The investor decides what to ask, and management decides how to respond. My responsibility is to give both sides a clear account of what the other person is saying. I want each to feel confident that their words are being heard accurately, even when they disagree about the business or its prospects.

I learned the importance of tone through a mistake. An investor was frustrated with a company's answers, and I carried that frustration into my interpretation. My delivery offended members of management. Since then, I have taken care to convey even difficult questions faithfully, in a steady voice. When a discussion gets heated, I sometimes slow down.

JII grew out of my writing about Japanese small-cap stocks on social media. Overseas investors who read my posts began asking for help speaking with companies. For smaller businesses without analyst coverage, getting that conversation started could be difficult. I began contacting companies, arranging meetings and interpreting them.

As those requests grew, I established Japan Investor Interface in 2023. Today, I arrange and interpret investor meetings and translate IR materials and corporate disclosures into English.

What matters to me is that companies and investors can have a useful conversation, each speaking from their own perspective. Understanding what investors want to know helps me support that exchange. It is work I want JII to continue doing with care.

Teddy Okuyama
Representative Director
Japan Investor Interface Co., Ltd.
