Message from the Founder

I still remember the first time I interpreted a meeting with overseas investors.

I was very nervous. In English, 80億円 is 8 billion yen. Today, I would not hesitate over that conversion, but back then, even getting the number of zeros right took concentration. It was all I could do to catch everything the speaker said and translate it as accurately as possible.

As I interpreted more meetings, I began to realize that speaking English and being able to interpret an investor meeting were two different things.

At the time, I did not fully understand why investors wanted to check the numbers in such detail.

Why did they want to know the profit margin of each segment? Why did they ask about sales growth, prices and volumes for individual products?

Investors use that information to build their own forecasts for the next 12 and 24 months. They also examine the assumptions behind the company's plans and compare them with their own views.

Translating analyst reports from securities firms and earnings briefings helped me understand how investors think.

When translating an earnings briefing, I would first listen to the recording from beginning to end before starting on the transcript. I would often listen to an entire hour-long briefing while walking around my neighborhood.

How did management speak? How did analysts frame their questions? What follow-up questions did the company's answers prompt?

I think I wanted to become familiar with the exchanges between analysts and management, rather than memorize individual questions.

After doing this repeatedly, I found it easier to understand the thinking behind questions in meetings with overseas investors as well.

I have now interpreted more than 300 meetings with overseas investors.

In these meetings, investors sometimes ask for details that do not appear in disclosure materials: segment profit margins, growth rates for key products, and the outlook for prices and volumes.

For example, if a company expects sales to increase next fiscal year, will that growth come from higher volumes, price increases or new products? If margins are expected to improve, will that come from a change in the product mix, lower costs or a smaller fixed-cost burden?

Investors work through those assumptions as they build their own earnings forecasts.

Japanese companies do not always provide that level of detail in their disclosures. In meetings, investors therefore ask about the factors behind the numbers as well as confirming what appears in the earnings presentation.

An interpreter can follow the exchange more accurately by understanding what the investor is trying to establish.

Understanding the purpose of an investor's question, however, is different from taking the investor's side.

When interpreting, I convey the speaker's words accurately, but try to keep their emotions out of my own voice.

In one meeting, an investor became frustrated with the company's answers. I conveyed that frustration in my tone as I translated the investor's words, and ended up offending members of management.

Since then, even when a question is tough, I have tried to convey its full content without adding any emotion of my own.

When a discussion becomes heated, I sometimes slow down a little as I interpret.

It is not the interpreter's role to challenge a company on an investor's behalf, or to persuade an investor on the company's behalf.

JII began with my posts about Japanese small-cap stocks on social media.

Overseas investors who had seen those posts began asking me, "I'd like to speak with this company."

Even when an overseas investor is interested in a company, arranging a direct conversation is not easy, particularly for small-cap stocks with no coverage from securities-firm analysts.

So I began contacting companies, requesting meetings and interpreting on the day.

As those requests grew, I established Japan Investor Interface Co., Ltd. in 2023.

Today, alongside arranging and interpreting meetings with overseas investors, I translate IR materials and corporate disclosures into English.

I want to understand what overseas investors need to know and help the conversation between a company and its investors flow as naturally as possible.

I want both sides to be able to speak openly from their own perspectives, without imposing investors' views on the company.

At JII, we intend to continue fulfilling that role with care.

Teddy Okuyama
Representative Director
Japan Investor Interface Co., Ltd.
