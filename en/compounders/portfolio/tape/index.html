Allocation Strategy

← Portfolio · conviction scoring lives on the Four Key Squares page
◆ The finding: 12-month outcomes depended mostly on the monthly trend. A daily dip was a good entry only while that trend was healthy. A price above both its daily and weekly bands did worse than average — that is where the portfolio trims.

A Bollinger band drapes two standard deviations around a stock's recent average price; "%B" says where today's price sits inside that band — 0 at the bottom, 1 at the top. We measured this on three clocks at once (daily, weekly, monthly) for every liquid Japanese stock, every day, for five years, then checked each position against the price 12 months later. Sorted by one question — where was the monthly trend, and what did the daily chart just do — the outcomes form a ladder:

Read it from the bottom up. A sharp one-day dip means opposite things in different trends: in a healthy monthly trend it was the best entry in the data; in a broken trend it did worse than the average stock. Nothing else we tested separated outcomes this widely. Every combination below:

The two matrices differ only in the monthly trend — that difference is the strategy. The gold ring is the best cell: a one-day dip while the weekly is still strong, inside a healthy monthly trend. The red ring is its opposite. Same move, opposite meaning.

From study to weights

The worked example. Take 7378 ASIRO. Every name starts equal. First, its Four Key Squares: still contested — not won, not lost — so the factor is 1.0. Second, valuation: among the holdings, ASIRO's price is the lowest against the profit its capital produces, so it gets the top factor, 1.15. Third, the bands: its current position belongs to a group that rose more often than average over the next 12 months — 1.11. Multiply: 1.0 × 1.15 × 1.11 ≈ 1.28. Scale every holding so the total is 100%, and ASIRO comes out at 6.80%.

The fine print. The study counts 2.4 million daily observations from 2021 to 2026, keeping only stocks that trade at least ¥10 million a day — stocks an investor could actually buy. Returns are prices only; dividends would have helped. The same stock counts once per day, so the samples overlap: large, but not independent. Weekly and monthly readings use finished bars only — nothing in the study looks into the future. The ladder and the matrices are averages of the cells shown, weighted by observations, and no cell under 1,000 observations stands on its own. These numbers describe the past five years. They do not promise the next five.

The data and the recipe. Prices are daily adjusted closes from J-Quants, the official data service of the Japan Exchange Group, for all listed stocks from June 2021 to June 2026. We kept stocks whose 20-day average trading value was ¥10 million or more. For each stock we built Bollinger bands — a 20-period average plus and minus two standard deviations — on daily bars, weekly bars (Friday closes), and monthly bars (month-end closes), and read %B as (price − lower band) ÷ (upper band − lower band). For every stock, every trading day, we recorded the three %B readings in 0.25 steps — weekly and monthly taken from the last finished bar — together with the adjusted close 252 trading days later (126, 378, and 504 days for the holding-period table). Every number on this page is a count or a median of those records. Anyone with the same data can rebuild it.

Who gets a place. The portfolio does not grow as coverage grows. Every published company gets the same three-factor score, and the twenty highest scores hold a place. One exception: a company whose valuation leaves the screen leaves the portfolio, whatever its score.

What the bands never decide. Whether a company is worth owning. That is decided on the Four Key Squares page, from filings. The bands decide two smaller things: how fast to build a position, and when to wait.
